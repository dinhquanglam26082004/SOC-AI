PLAYBOOK ID: PB-DOS-001

NAME:
Suspected Denial of Service Response

Provenance: Curated summary of MITRE ATT&CK T1498 (Network Denial of Service,
sub-techniques Direct Network Flood T1498.001 and Reflection Amplification
T1498.002), T1499 (Endpoint Denial of Service), mitigation M1037, and the
CISA/FBI/MS-ISAC guidance on understanding and responding to DDoS attacks,
reviewed 2026-11-19. Sources: https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1498/ ,
https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1499/ , https://attack.mitre.org/mitigations/M1037/
and https://www.cisa.gov/ (search "Understanding and Responding to DDoS Attacks").
Guidance, not incident evidence.

TRIGGER:
Network or application telemetry shows an unusual increase in requests,
connections, or failed flows targeting a service. A threshold alert is a
signal to investigate, not proof that the service is unavailable or that
the traffic is malicious. This playbook applies to suspected DoS and
possible DDoS activity. MITRE maps network floods to T1498 (Impact, TA0040):
T1498.001 Direct Network Flood from one or many sources, and T1498.002
Reflection Amplification, which abuses third-party servers that respond to a
spoofed victim address. DoS aimed at the host itself is T1499.

INVESTIGATION:
1. Identify the affected service, destination, port, protocol, detection
   window, observed count, configured threshold, and available source count.
2. Confirm service impact with the service owner using health checks,
   latency, error rates, resource utilization, and user reports.
3. Compare the observation with the service's normal baseline, expected
   business traffic, scheduled jobs, and recent deployments or load tests.
4. Review relevant load balancer, firewall, application, and network
   telemetry for request patterns and whether traffic reached the service.
5. Check sensor health, capture loss, event backlog, aggregation limits,
   and time synchronization. Counts may represent observed flows or
   requests, not packets or unique clients.
6. Assess whether traffic comes from one source or many. NAT, proxies,
   spoofed addresses, and shared infrastructure can make source counts
   misleading; multiple sources alone do not prove a distributed attack.
   Source spoofing and reflection reduce the value of simple source-IP
   filtering, so prefer behavior-based assessment over fixed source lists.
7. Preserve the relevant alerts, logs, service-health measurements, and
   timeline before making changes.
8. Record the assessment as suspected or confirmed service impact, and
   document remaining uncertainty. Do not infer attacker identity or intent
   from traffic volume alone.

RESPONSE:
1. If service impact is not corroborated, continue monitoring and gather
   service-owner and telemetry evidence; do not block sources solely because
   a threshold alert fired.
2. If impact is confirmed, coordinate mitigation with the service owner and
   network/hosting provider. Prefer scoped, reversible controls such as
   rate limits, filtering for a verified traffic pattern, or provider
   scrubbing where available (M1037 Filter Network Traffic).
3. Before applying a control, identify its target, duration, expected effect,
   customer impact, monitoring criteria, and rollback procedure.
4. Avoid broad IP, country, or network-range blocks unless an analyst and
   service owner validate the scope and business impact.
5. Monitor service health and legitimate traffic after mitigation; roll back
   or adjust controls if they disrupt expected service.
6. Preserve incident evidence and record decisions, approvals, changes,
   timestamps, and observed outcomes.
7. After recovery, follow the CISA/FBI/MS-ISAC guidance: watch for a secondary
   attack, update the DoS response plan, and refresh the baseline of normal
   network activity. Review capacity, upstream protections, and alert
   thresholds with the service owner.

ESCALATION:
Escalate promptly to the SOC lead and affected service owner when availability
impact is confirmed, critical services are affected, or mitigation requires
provider or production changes. Engage the network/hosting provider for
upstream traffic mitigation. Escalate to incident management when impact is
prolonged, widespread, or affects critical business operations.

Human approval from the incident lead and affected service owner is required
before production traffic filtering, rate-limit changes, service isolation,
or other controls that may affect legitimate users. No response action is
executed by this playbook.
